# Offline MusicBrainz Layer Study and Artist Vocabulary Proposal

Observation date: September 18, 2026. MusicBrainz snapshot: `20260912-002318`.

This study evaluates whether an offline MusicBrainz snapshot can improve useful instrumental results without presenting contextual metadata as proof about a specific recording. It extends the initial [Instrumental Coverage Study](01.md) with five contemporary-biased profiles and one historical profile selected to include more MPB, samba, and jazz.

The published study anonymizes profile handles as `Contemporary A` through `Contemporary E` and `Historical`. Artist names, track titles, and MusicBrainz identifiers are omitted. The historical profile uses the Last.fm six-month period; its overall ranking was rejected for this study because it did not represent the recent listening variety that the cohort was intended to test.

## Research questions

The study tested three possible enrichment layers:

1. **Track evidence**: an instrument relationship scoped to the listened recording or track.
2. **Release context**: an instrument credit associated with the matching release but not safely attributable to the listened track.
3. **Artist catalog recurrence**: an instrument documented on at least three distinct recordings credited to an artist present in the listening history.

The first layer can support the existing instrumental palette. The second and third are contextual evidence and must not be promoted to track evidence.

The central question was whether either contextual layer provides enough useful information to offer an honest experience when strict track evidence is sparse.

## Protocol

The experiment used an offline extraction of the MusicBrainz full dump rather than the public API. This removes transient `503` responses and shared-egress IP behavior from the measurement. It does not remove catalog incompleteness or uneven credit practices.

For each profile, the study analyzed 50 Last.fm top tracks. The five contemporary samples used `period=overall`. The historical sample used `period=6month` and contained 859 plays across its 50 tracks.

Identity resolution kept MusicBrainz tracks and recordings separate and used the following paths:

1. interpret the Last.fm MBID as a recording when valid;
2. resolve a MusicBrainz track MBID to its recording;
3. use conservative artist, title, and album matching;
4. retain ambiguous and unresolved identities instead of choosing a convenient candidate.

Instrument names were normalized through the project's instrument mapping. Results were measured independently by track count and play count. `Voice` was also removed in a control measurement to determine whether vocal credits alone were inflating instrumental coverage.

Release expansion used the release referenced by `medium.release`. This matters because the dump columns are `id`, `release`, and `position`; reading `position` as the release identifier silently prevents valid release matching. The ETL must preserve and test this mapping before release-context results are reproduced.

## Historical sample

The six-month historical sample resolved 41 of 50 tracks. Release context could be determined for 32 tracks: 23 through an exact track MBID and nine through conservative album matching.

| Layer                       | Tracks reached |      Plays reached | Artists represented |
| --------------------------- | -------------: | -----------------: | ------------------: |
| Direct track evidence       |   5 / 50 (10%) | 183 / 859 (21.30%) |                   5 |
| Direct plus release context |   7 / 50 (14%) | 206 / 859 (23.98%) |                   6 |
| Recurrent artist catalog    |  40 / 50 (80%) | 753 / 859 (87.66%) |                  26 |
| Any mapped layer            |  41 / 50 (82%) | 763 / 859 (88.82%) |                  27 |

Removing `voice` did not change direct coverage: all five directly covered tracks also had non-vocal instrument evidence.

The direct layer documented instruments including double bass, drums, guitar, piano, saxophone, trombone, and trumpet. Release context added useful but narrow detail, including cuica, flute, percussion, and additional horns. The recurrent catalog layer exposed a much broader vocabulary consistent with the cohort, including piano, double bass, drums, acoustic and classical guitars, cavaquinho, percussion, vibraphone, brass, and several woodwind families.

This qualitative coherence is encouraging, but it is not proof that those instruments occur in every listened track by those artists.

## Findings

### Direct evidence remains too sparse for a universal palette

The strict layer is semantically strong but uneven. The historical profile exceeded 20% play coverage while reaching only 10% of tracks. Two contemporary samples approached one threshold without satisfying the other. This supports the existing rule that track and play coverage are conjunctive and cannot compensate for each other.

Sparse direct evidence is not just a resolution problem. Many successfully resolved recordings have no usable recording- or track-scoped instrument relationship.

### Release expansion is not the missing coverage layer

Release context occasionally contributes valuable details, but its quantitative effect was small. In the historical sample it increased reach from five to seven tracks; across the original pooled scan it was present on two tracks and increased the union by only one.

Release credits should therefore remain visible context with provenance. They should not count toward strict coverage and should not be copied onto every recording in a release.

### Recurrent artist catalogs provide a different useful object

Artist catalog recurrence changes the result substantially. It performs well for the historical cohort and for several contemporary profiles, but it answers a different question:

> Which instrumental families recur in the documented work of the artists this person listens to?

It does not answer:

> Which instruments are documented in the exact recordings this person played?

Treating the first answer as the second would inflate coverage and create false certainty. Keeping it separate enables a useful result without weakening the evidence contract.

### A raw catalog union is too broad

The historical sample produced more than 40 mapped instrument names even after requiring recurrence on three recordings. Long careers, reissues, orchestral collaborations, and unusually detailed sessions can dominate such a union.

The result therefore needs family aggregation, prevalence, artist balancing, and explicit provenance. Recurrence alone is a useful noise floor, not a complete ranking method.

## Product proposal: Artist Vocabulary

Introduce **Artist Vocabulary** as a new contextual analysis alongside the existing track-evidence palette.

Artist Vocabulary describes recurring instrument families in the documented catalogs of artists represented in a listening history. It must always be labeled as artist-level context. It must never increase `analysis.coverage_tracks`, `analysis.coverage_plays`, or the confidence of a recording-level claim.

The product should distinguish these experiences:

| Available evidence                                                                         | Primary result                                    | Optional or fallback result                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Track evidence passes the palette gate                                                     | Instrumental palette based on listened recordings | Artist Vocabulary as a separate complementary lens                                     |
| Some track evidence exists but fails the palette gate; artist evidence passes its own gate | Partial documented-track summary                  | Artist Vocabulary as the useful contextual result                                      |
| No usable track evidence; artist evidence passes its own gate                              | No recording palette                              | Artist Vocabulary only, with an explicit contextual label                              |
| Neither layer passes its gate                                                              | Insufficient evidence                             | Coverage status and an invitation to try another listening period; no inferred palette |

This preserves a stronger experience for users with sufficient track evidence while providing an honest alternative for users whose histories are poorly documented at recording level. Users who have a palette can compare “documented in your tracks” with “recurring across these artists' catalogs”; users without a palette can still explore a defensible musical context.

The two results must not be merged into a single score. A contextual family can appear in Artist Vocabulary without appearing in the track palette, and the interface should explain why.

## Candidate eligibility and ranking

The experiment used a minimum of three distinct documented recordings per artist and instrument. That rule is a candidate starting point, not a production threshold established by this six-profile study.

A future vocabulary gate should evaluate at least:

- the number of listened artists with qualifying catalog evidence;
- track reach: the share of listened tracks whose credited artists have a qualifying vocabulary;
- play reach: the corresponding share of plays;
- recurrence on distinct recordings, not duplicate releases of one recording;
- prevalence: qualifying recordings divided by the artist's documented recordings in scope;
- concentration: whether one artist supplies most of the result.

The contextual metrics should be named `track_reach` and `play_reach`, not coverage. Coverage remains reserved for accepted evidence about listened recordings.

A candidate family score can be structured as:

```text
artist contribution = listening weight for artist
                    × documented family prevalence for artist
                    × evidence-quality weight

vocabulary family score = sum of capped artist contributions
```

The calculation should group specific instruments into reviewed families before profile aggregation, deduplicate recordings, cap each artist's contribution, and retain drill-down provenance. Instrument-level details may be shown beneath a family only when their recurrence independently satisfies the vocabulary rule.

Before adoption, thresholds should be calibrated on a larger and more varied cohort. At minimum, tests should include contemporary electronic production, rap and sample-heavy music, orchestral catalogs, traditional repertories, compilations, collaborations, and artists with very large discographies.

## Evidence and interface requirements

Every Artist Vocabulary result should disclose:

- that it describes documented work by listened artists, not guaranteed instrumentation of the listened tracks;
- the MusicBrainz snapshot and vocabulary-methodology versions;
- qualifying artists and recording counts behind each family;
- track reach, play reach, and artist diversity;
- missing and unresolved identities;
- that absent metadata does not prove an instrument is absent.

Suggested interface labels are:

- **In your tracks** for the existing strict palette;
- **Across these artists** for Artist Vocabulary;
- **Not enough documented evidence** when neither analysis is eligible.

Copy should avoid “your music contains” for vocabulary results. Safer language is “these instruments recur in documented recordings by artists in this listening period.”

Artist Vocabulary must not unlock recording-dependent features such as exact-track discoveries or claims about co-occurrence within a listened recording. Any editorial interpretation derived from vocabulary would need its own rules and version rather than reusing the current palette temperament.

## Operational implications

The result weakens the case for repeatedly producing a narrow per-recording derivative from the full dump. That process paid the cost of decompressing and scanning the dump while still returning very few recording credits. A vocabulary-capable derivative should instead be organized around reusable artist-to-recording and recording-to-instrument relationships, then queried for the artists present in a profile.

This does not by itself select a storage format or update strategy. A separate architecture experiment should compare a reusable indexed snapshot, partitioned derivatives, and incremental MusicBrainz replication. Its benchmark must include build time, compressed size, R2 storage and reads, CI runtime, query latency, and the number of profiles served per snapshot.

The offline approach solves availability and rate-limit exposure. Artist Vocabulary addresses usefulness under sparse track-scoped metadata. They are related but separate problems, and both need to be solved without changing the meaning of evidence.

## Decision

The study supports continuing with Artist Vocabulary as a separately gated product concept. It does not support counting artist-catalog or release-level relationships as evidence for listened recordings.

Before implementation:

1. preserve and test the ETL's `medium.release` mapping;
2. define a versioned Artist Vocabulary schema and provenance model;
3. calibrate recurrence, prevalence, diversity, and concentration gates on a larger cohort;
4. validate a reusable offline index architecture against CI and R2 constraints;
5. add regression cases proving that contextual evidence never changes strict track coverage.
