# Instrumental Coverage Study

Observation date: September 12, 2026.

This is an anonymized summary of a five-profile Last.fm study. The published version uses `Sample A` through `Sample E` and omits profile handles, artist names, track titles, and recording identifiers.

## Objective

The study measured catalog coverage before setting methodology thresholds. It separated three stages:

1. listening-track identity availability;
2. resolution to a canonical MusicBrainz recording;
3. presence of a recording- or track-level instrumental relationship.

A resolved recording without an accepted instrumental relationship was not considered covered. Artist-level associations, genre tags, composition credits, and general release credits were not treated as recording evidence.

## Collection protocol

For each sample, the study queried the first 50 results of Last.fm `user.getTopTracks` with `period=overall`. Identity resolution used this order:

1. look up a Last.fm MBID as a MusicBrainz recording;
2. if the MBID identified a track, resolve it through `tid` to one recording;
3. fall back to a conservative artist-and-title search with normalized-field validation;
4. fetch the recording and inspect its `artist-recording` instrument relationships.

The protocol preserves the distinction between MusicBrainz tracks and recordings. Only direct recording or track relationships were eligible for strict instrumental coverage. Release-level credits remained contextual unless they had preserved recording or track scope.

The results describe the Last.fm and MusicBrainz data available on the observation date. Both services and their records may change.

## Observed bias

- Four samples were dominated by contemporary rap, trap, or hip-hop and internet-oriented scenes; one had stronger indie/pop-rock, pop, and K-pop representation.
- There was limited representation of classical, traditional folk, orchestral, historical, and other older repertories.
- Of the 156 resolved recordings, 138 (88.5%) were released after 2010 and only two were released before 2000. The study therefore does not establish coverage for older catalogs.
- MBID availability ranged from 28% to 78% across samples; direct instrumental coverage ranged from 4% to 20% of tracks.
- Identity resolution was more reliable for popular or well-cataloged releases. Instrumental evidence was more common for bands and sessions with conventional personnel credits.
- Generic relationships such as `additional`, and credits without explicit recording scope, could not support specific instrument claims.

The results indicate a double bias: popular cataloged releases are easier to identify, while conventional credit practices make their instrumentation easier to document. A high-coverage sample must not be used as the expected baseline for every listener.

## Transient failures

MusicBrainz returned `503` responses during part of the collection. The process made 60 additional attempts and preserved valid responses in temporary cache. A targeted recheck produced no additional accepted instrumental credits.

Service unavailability was not counted as evidence, and it was not treated as definitive evidence that a recording had no credits.

## Methodology implications

The study supports the following safeguards, now encoded in [Analysis Methodology](../methodology.md):

- evaluate track coverage and play coverage together;
- require absolute track floors and artist diversity in addition to percentages;
- keep identity resolution separate from instrumental evidence;
- reject release-only credits, generic relationships, and unmapped names as specific instrument evidence;
- expose `partial` or `insufficient` results instead of filling gaps with assumptions;
- gate discovery, sound balance, and temperament independently;
- version every change to thresholds, weighting, or interpretation rules.

The current implementation uses methodology version `0.3.0`. It retains the coverage safeguards introduced after this study and adds deterministic recurrence rules for temperament identities.
